# Scam First Aid — free, private first steps

**Operation Help Humanity · AiNUNNAKI**

If you received a suspicious message, clicked a link, or lost money to a scam, start here. **You do not need to share any personal information with us.** This guide is available for free without an account or AI API.

> **Please note:** This is general educational guidance—not a definitive scam detector or personalized legal, financial, or technical advice. A familiar company name or logo doesn't prove a message is legitimate. If anyone is in immediate physical danger, contact local emergency services.

## Stop, verify, protect

- **Don't use the suspicious message's links, attachments, or contact numbers.**
- **Verify independently.** Open the organization's official app, a known bookmark, or call a number from a statement or card.
- **Don't share passwords or one-time security codes.** A demand for immediate action is a reason to slow down.
- **No promise of recovery is reliable.** Be cautious of anyone charging upfront to recover scam losses.

## If you haven't clicked anything

Don't respond or click. Verify separately if needed. Report the message through your email/text service. In the United States, you can also report at **[ReportFraud.ftc.gov](https://reportfraud.ftc.gov/)**, and the FTC recommends forwarding phishing texts to 7726 (SPAM).

## If you clicked a link or opened an attachment

Stop entering information. Update your device security software and run a scan. If you entered a password, change it via the official website from a trusted device and turn on multifactor authentication. Check your accounts for suspicious activity. If you installed software or granted remote control, get trusted technical support.

## If you sent money or shared bank/card information

**Immediately** contact the bank, card issuer, or payment service using its official contact method. Explain what happened and ask about stopping the payment, recovering funds, and protecting the account. Save receipts and evidence—but don't post full account details publicly.

FTC guidance explains options that vary by payment method: **[What to do if you were scammed](https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/what-do-if-you-were-scammed)**. Recovery is not guaranteed.

## If you gave someone a password or login code

Use the real service to change your password and end other sessions when possible. Turn on multifactor authentication, secure the connected email account, and replace any reused passwords. Inspect recovery contact details and recent sign-ins.

**[CISA: How to turn on multifactor authentication](https://www.cisa.gov/secure-our-world/turn-mfa)**

## If you shared sensitive identity information

In the U.S., visit **[IdentityTheft.gov](https://www.identitytheft.gov/)**, the official FTC recovery service, for a plan tailored to the information exposed. Contact affected institutions directly. Outside the U.S., use your country's official consumer protection or identity theft service.
